FlashAttention, built up from first principles

Four parts, in the order the idea actually has to be understood. First what attention computes. Then softmax, and the single structural property that will cause all the trouble. Then where the cost actually lives — which is not where everyone was looking. Only then FlashAttention, which at that point is the obvious move.

Part one

What attention computes

Three lines. Score, normalize, average. Two of the three are matrix multiplies; the middle one is where the entire paper happens.

01the operation
Attention
S=QK⊤,P=softmax(S),O=PV
Q,K,V,O∈RN×dS,P∈RN×N
N = sequence length, d = head dimension, and softmax is applied row-wise.

"Standard attention implementations materialize the matrices S and P to HBM, which takes O(N2) memory. Often N≫d (e.g., for GPT2, N=1024 and d=64)."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §2.2, p. 4
Reading

Look at the shapes. Everything supplied and everything returned is N×d. The only N×N objects are S and P, and each appears on the left of one assignment and immediately on the right of the next. They are pure intermediates — nobody asked for them.

And N≫d is structural, not incidental: head dimension is kept small so many heads fit, while sequence length is the axis everyone wants to grow.

Per head per sample, fp16, d=64:

1 024512 KiB4 MiB8×

Every doubling of context doubles the ratio. At 16K, one head of one sample wants a gibibyte of scratch — 12 heads and a batch of 8 is 96 GiB on a 40 GB card. Long context was not slow; it did not fit.

02one output row
Row i of the output
si=[qi⋅k1,…,qi⋅kN]∈RN
pi=softmax(si),Oi=∑j=1Npijvj
N scores in, N weights out, one weighted average of the value vectors. What softmax does to that vector is Part 2 — here it is a black box.
Reading

Attention is N independent copies of this. Row i never looks at row i′ — that is what "row-wise" in ¶01 means, and it is why the query axis parallelizes for free.

Stacking all N of these score vectors si is what produces the N×N matrix S. Nothing yet forces it to exist all at once.

One row, four keys. Scores s=[2,1,4,1], values v=[10,20,30,40] (1-D for legibility).

p=softmax([2,1,4,1])=[0.1096,0.0403,0.8098,0.0403]
O=0.1096(10)+0.0403(20)+0.8098(30)+0.0403(40)=27.81

The answer sits near v3=30 because key 3 scored highest. (The softmax arithmetic itself is ¶04.) Remember 27.81 — ¶06 destroys it.

03two quadratic terms, not one
The distinction the whole paper rests on
CostDepends onReducible?
ComputeO(N2d)what is computedonly by approximating

Reading

"Attention is quadratic" was treated as one fact with one fix — approximate it. But the two terms behave completely differently. You cannot score all pairs with fewer than N2 dot products; you can avoid ever writing the N×N array down.

FlashAttention leaves the compute term alone — Theorem 1 confirms O(N2d) FLOPs — and eliminates the memory term. This is why it can be exact and still win.

Part two

Softmax

One definition, one identity, one obstacle. The identity is the lever; the obstacle is what it has to lift.

04the definition
Softmax
softmax(x)i=exi∑kexk
…computed in the numerically stable form
m(x)≔maxixi,f(x)≔[ex1−m(x)…exB−m(x)],ℓ(x)≔∑if(x)i,softmax(x)≔f(x)ℓ(x)
Weights are positive, sum to one, and preserve the ordering of the scores.

"For numerical stability, the softmax of vector x∈RB is computed as …"

arXiv:2205.14135 · §3.1 Tiling, p. 4
Reading

Why subtract the max. fp16 tops out at 65 504, so ex overflows once x>11.09 — well inside the range raw attention scores reach. After subtraction every exponent is ≤0, so every term lands in (0,1]. Overflow becomes impossible by construction.

The tell that this is more than hygiene: m(x) and ℓ(x) are given names and treated as outputs, not buried inside one expression. They are about to become carried state.

05the lever — shift invariance
The reference point is arbitrary
softmax(x+c)=softmax(x)for any constant c
…so it can be changed after the fact, by one scalar
emold−mnew⋅exi−mold=exi−mnew
A maximum only ever increases, so mnew≥mold and the factor always lies in (0,1] — it can only scale down, never overflow.
Reading

The first line is the standard stability identity. The second is the one the whole algorithm is built on: if you exponentiated relative to mold and later learn the true reference is mnew, you do not revisit the scores — one multiplication re-bases an entire block.

Check on the ¶02 numbers. With no subtraction, s=[2,1,4,1] gives ℓraw=67.423 and p3=54.598/67.423=0.80978. With m=4 subtracted, p3=1/1.2349=0.80978.

Identical — and note 67.423=e4×1.2349. The shift scaled numerator and denominator by the same e4, and the ratio never noticed.

Deferred, retroactive correction by a single scalar. ¶20 is this identity applied to blocks; Algorithm 1 line 12 is it applied to an accumulator.

"Tiling. We compute attention by blocks. Softmax couples columns of K, so we decompose the large softmax with scaling [51, 60, 66]."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §3.1 Tiling, p. 4
Reading

Nine words in exactly the right coordinates. Rows of Q are independent, so splitting Q needs no new mathematics. Columns of K are coupled, because the denominator runs across them — so splitting K and V is the hard part.

If you must see a whole row before emitting any part of it, and rows have length N, you are holding an N-wide object — and N of those is N×N. That is where the big matrix comes from.

Take the ¶02 row — output 27.81 — and let a fifth key arrive with score 8, value 50. The max jumps 4 → 8, so everything re-bases:

The obstacle and its solution are one fact seen from two sides. Late information invalidates old weights — but only by a uniform scalar, so old work can be repaired instead of redone.
Part three

Where the problem actually is

Everything so far is textbook. Now the paper's real contribution begins — not a faster algorithm, but the claim that the field had been measuring the wrong thing, and that the cost lives somewhere nobody was looking.

3a · the diagnosis

The proxy metric was wrong

Approximate attention reduced FLOPs, provably. It frequently did not get faster. The paper opens by explaining why those two statements are compatible.

07§1 · the accusation

"Although these methods reduce the compute requirements to linear or near-linear in sequence length, many of them do not display wall-clock speedup against standard attention and have not gained wide adoption. One main reason is that they focus on FLOP reduction (which may not correlate with wall-clock speed) and tend to ignore overheads from memory access (IO)."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §1 Introduction, p. 1
Reading

The most important five words in the introduction are the parenthetical: "which may not correlate with wall-clock speed". That is a direct attack on the field's default proxy metric.

FLOP count is a perfectly good cost model when arithmetic is the scarce resource. The claim is that on modern accelerators, for this operation, it simply is not — so an algorithm can be provably better in FLOPs and observably worse on hardware. "Have not gained wide adoption" is the evidence offered: practitioners voted with their code.

Everything that follows is an attempt to replace the proxy, not to optimize harder against it.

This is not a claim that approximate attention is useless. It is a claim that the metric used to justify it was not predictive. §5 of the paper goes on to build block-sparse FlashAttention — approximate attention redone under the new metric.
08§1 · why the proxy broke

"On modern GPUs, compute speed has out-paced memory speed [61, 62, 63], and most operations in Transformers are bottlenecked by memory accesses [43]."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §1 Introduction, p. 2
Reading

A hardware-trend argument, not an argument about attention. Arithmetic throughput has grown faster than memory bandwidth for decades, so the number of operations you can afford per byte fetched keeps rising — and more and more operations fall on the wrong side of it.

Read "most operations in Transformers" literally. Not attention alone: layer norm, GELU, dropout, residual adds, softmax. The matmuls are the only comfortably compute-bound part, and they are a minority of the operation count even while dominating the FLOP count.

Step 1 — where the machine balances. Put the paper's §2.1 figure (HBM at 1.5 TB/s) against an A100's fp16 tensor-core peak of 312 TFLOP/s (a datasheet number, not from the paper):

312×1012FLOP/s1.5×1012B/s≈208FLOP per byte

Anything below 208 FLOP/byte cannot keep the arithmetic units fed — it is waiting on memory.

Step 2 — where the 4 bytes come from. Softmax reads the score matrix and writes the probability matrix. In fp16 each element is 2 bytes, so per element:

HBM traffic per elementBytes
read sij2
write pij2
total4 B

4 bytes is the optimistic floor — it assumes one fused pass. A textbook three-pass safe softmax (find the max, then the sum, then normalize) re-reads the row twice more and moves 8 B per element.

Step 3 — where the 5 flops come from. Walk the stable softmax of ¶04, counting the operations each element is subjected to:

Step 4 — the verdict.

A factor of ~170 short. And the conclusion does not depend on the exact count: even valuing exp at 10 flops — it is a polynomial approximation in a special-function unit, not one instruction — gives 14/4=3.5 FLOP/byte, still ~60× short. There is no plausible accounting under which softmax is compute-bound.

09§1 · why nobody had fixed it

"IO-aware algorithms have been critical for similar memory-bound operations… such as database joins [71], image processing [70], numerical linear algebra [4], and more [40, 85]. However, common Python interfaces to deep learning such as PyTorch and Tensorflow do not allow fine-grained control of memory access."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §1 Introduction, p. 2
Reading

Two moves in one paragraph. First, this is not a new idea — IO-awareness is standard practice in database engines, image pipelines and BLAS. The paper is importing a mature principle, which is a far easier claim to defend than inventing one.

Second, the explanation for why deep learning missed it is tooling, not ignorance. In PyTorch, softmax(q @ k.T) @ v is three library calls. Each is a separate GPU kernel, and each kernel's contract is to read its inputs from device memory and write its outputs back. There is no way to say "keep that intermediate on the chip" — the abstraction boundary that makes the framework usable is exactly the boundary that forces the round trip.

This sentence is why the paper ends up writing CUDA. The optimization is not expressible at the framework level; it needs control the framework deliberately does not expose.
3b · the hardware

What the machine actually charges for

§2.1 reads like background. It is not — it is the cost model the rest of the paper optimizes against.

10§2.1 · the memory hierarchy

"The A100 GPU has 40-80GB of high bandwidth memory (HBM) with bandwidth 1.5-2.0TB/s and 192KB of on-chip SRAM per each of 108 streaming multiprocessors with bandwidth estimated around 19TB/s. The on-chip SRAM is an order of magnitude faster than HBM but many orders of magnitude smaller in size."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §2.1 Hardware Performance, p. 3
Reading

The whole paper is a response to the asymmetry in that last sentence, so it is worth making it numeric.

≈12.7×
SRAM bandwidth advantage (19 vs 1.5 TB/s)
≈2000×
HBM capacity advantage (40 GB vs 20 MB aggregate)
192 KB
SRAM per SM — the real working-set limit
108
streaming multiprocessors on an A100

Figure 1's pyramid gives the aggregate: SRAM 19 TB/s (20 MB) over HBM 1.5 TB/s (40 GB) over DRAM 12.8 GB/s (>1 TB). Note that 20 MB is 192 KB×108 — a sum across SMs, not a shared pool. No kernel gets 20 MB; it gets 192 KB.

The bandwidth gap is about one order of magnitude, which sounds modest. The capacity gap is three. That mismatch is the design problem: SRAM is fast enough to be worth using and small enough that nothing interesting fits. An algorithm that wants SRAM must be restructured until its working set is tiny.
11§2.1 · the execution model defines the unit of cost

"Execution Model. GPUs have a massive number of threads to execute an operation (called a kernel). Each kernel loads inputs from HBM to registers and SRAM, computes, then writes outputs to HBM."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §2.1, p. 3
Reading

Twenty-three words that quietly determine the entire paper. This is not a description of good practice — it is the contract. A kernel that produces a value another kernel needs must put that value in HBM. SRAM does not persist across kernel launches; there is no other channel.

The corollary: the number of kernels is a lower bound on the number of HBM round trips. That turns "how many library calls does my attention use" into a performance question.

It also explains the shape of the standard implementation. S = Q @ K.T writes S to HBM not because anyone wants it there, but because the kernel has no other way to hand it to softmax.

Read this as the definition of the currency Part 4 spends. Everything from here is measured in kernel boundaries crossed.
12§2.1 · softmax lands on the wrong list

"…operations can be classified as either compute-bound or memory-bound. This is commonly measured by the arithmetic intensity [85], which is the number of arithmetic operations per byte of memory access.

1. Compute-bound: … Typical examples are matrix multiply with large inner dimension, and convolution with large number of channels.

2. Memory-bound: … Examples include most other operations: elementwise (e.g., activation, dropout), and reduction (e.g., sum, softmax, batch norm, layer norm)."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §2.1, p. 3
Reading

The taxonomy is standard; the payload is which list softmax lands on. It is named explicitly as memory-bound — and softmax is the middle third of attention.

So attention is not one kind of operation. It is a compute-bound matmul, then a memory-bound reduction over an N×N array, then another compute-bound matmul. Optimizing the matmuls harder does nothing for the middle. That structural fact is why attention resisted the usual tricks, and why the fix had to change the dataflow rather than the kernel.

Now apply the same accounting per stage at N=4096, d=64, fp16 (balance point ≈208 FLOP/B; softmax uses the 5-FLOP / 4-byte-per-element derivation from ¶08):

StageFLOPsBytes movedIntensityVerdict
S=QK⊤2.1 G33 MiB≈62compute-leaning
P=softmax(S)≈0.08 G64 MiB≈1.2memory-bound
O=PV2.1 G33 MiB≈60compute-leaning

The softmax does 2.5% of the arithmetic and moves as many bytes as both matmuls combined. No amount of arithmetic cleverness helps — only not moving those bytes.

3c · the bill

What standard attention actually spends

With the cost model in place, the four-line reference implementation can be read for what it is.

13Algorithm 0 · an IO ledger disguised as pseudocode
Algorithm 0 — Standard Attention Implementation
Require: Matrices Q, K, V ∈ ℝN×d in HBM.
1:Load Q, K by blocks from HBM, compute S = QK⊤, write S to HBM.
2:Read S from HBM, compute P = softmax(S), write P to HBM.
3:Load P and V by blocks from HBM, compute O = PV, write O to HBM.
4:Return O.
arXiv:2205.14135 · Algorithm 0, p. 4
Reading

Read this as a bank statement. Almost every clause is a transfer; the arithmetic is mentioned in passing — "compute S=QK⊤" occupies four words between two HBM operations. The paper is showing you the algorithm in the currency it has just defined.

Three lines, three kernels, three unavoidable round trips (¶11). And notice that line 1 already says "by blocks". The standard implementation is tiled — matmul kernels have always blocked for shared memory. Blocking was never the missing piece. What is missing is that each kernel still deposits its complete output in HBM before the next may start.

The ledger at N=4096, d=64, one head, fp16 (S,P are 32 MiB each; Q,K,V,O are 512 KiB each):

LineHBM trafficBytes
1read Q, K · write S1 + 32 MiB
2read S · write P32 + 32 MiB
3read P, V · write O32.5 + 0.5 MiB
totalof which 128 MiB is pure scratch130 MiB

The useful payload — what the caller supplied and received — is 2 MiB. 98.5% of the traffic exists only to move temporaries between kernels. At 1.5 TB/s that is ≈91 µs, against ≈14 µs of matmul at peak. The arithmetic was never the problem.

14§2.2 · and it gets worse in practice

"As some or most of the operations are memory-bound (e.g., softmax), the large number of memory accesses translates to slow wall-clock time.

This problem is exacerbated by other elementwise operations applied to the attention matrix, such as masking applied to S or dropout applied to P. As a result, there have been many attempts to fuse several elementwise operations, such as fusing masking with softmax [77]."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §2.2, p. 4
Reading

Here the threads join. §2.1 established that reductions and elementwise ops are memory-bound; §2.2 established that attention builds an N×N array; therefore attention runs memory-bound operations over the largest object in the layer.

The second paragraph is the paper being scrupulous. Masking and dropout are not in the textbook formula but are in every real implementation, and each adds a full read-modify-write pass over the big matrix.

At N=4096, fp16, the attention matrix is 32 MiB. An unfused mask → softmax → dropout pipeline touches it six times:

  • mask: read 32 MiB, write 32 MiB
  • softmax: read 32 MiB, write 32 MiB
  • dropout: read 32 MiB, write 32 MiB

192 MiB of HBM traffic for three operations doing maybe a dozen flops per element — ≈134 µs at 1.5 TB/s, while the two matmuls that are the attention total 4×40962×64≈4.3 GFLOP, about 14 µs. The bookkeeping costs roughly ten times the mathematics.

Existing fusion work removed some passes over the big matrix. FlashAttention removes the big matrix. That is a difference in kind.
15§2.1 · why the obvious fix is not enough

"Kernel fusion. The most common approach to accelerate memory-bound operations is kernel fusion: if there are multiple operations applied to the same input, the input can be loaded once from HBM, instead of multiple times for each operation. …

However, in the context of model training, the intermediate values still need to be written to HBM to save for the backward pass, reducing the effectiveness of naive kernel fusion."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §2.1, pp. 3–4
Reading

This is the pivot of the whole background section, and it is easy to read past because it is phrased as a limitation rather than a motivation.

Fusion is the obvious fix, and the paper concedes it works — for inference. Then it points out that training breaks it. Autograd needs S and P to compute gradients, so even if the fused kernel never needed them in HBM, the backward pass does. Fusion saved the reads and kept every one of the writes.

So: fusion alone is not sufficient, and the reason is the backward pass. That makes recomputation not a clever bonus but a logical necessity — the only way to satisfy the backward pass without writing the matrices is to not keep them and rebuild them later.

§2 does not end with "and so we fuse the kernel". It ends with "fusion is not enough, and here is exactly why" — which is what makes Part 4's second technique unavoidable rather than optional.
Part four

FlashAttention

Everything is now in place. Part 2 showed that softmax refuses to be computed in pieces — but only by a uniform scalar. Part 3 showed the cost is bytes moved, not operations performed. Put those together and the algorithm is almost forced.

16Abstract · the thesis, and the word "exact"

"We argue that a missing principle is making attention algorithms IO-aware—accounting for reads and writes between levels of GPU memory. We propose FlashAttention, an IO-aware exact attention algorithm that uses tiling to reduce the number of memory reads/writes between GPU high bandwidth memory (HBM) and GPU on-chip SRAM."

arXiv:2205.14135 · Abstract, p. 1
Reading

"A missing principle" — not a missing optimization or a missing kernel. A principle is something that governs how you design the algorithm, the way FLOP count governed the previous generation. And "between levels" means movement, not footprint: a byte that never leaves the chip is free by this metric even though it very much exists.

"Exact" is the single word that separates this paper from the entire line of work Part 3a dismissed. The output is the same attention — not an approximation, not a low-rank surrogate, not a sparsified variant. There is no quality axis to trade on because nothing was traded. Part 1 ¶03 explains why that is even possible: only the memory term was ever schedule-dependent.

And note what tiling is claimed to reduce: the number of reads/writes. Not operations, not peak footprint — the count of transfers across one specific boundary. The abstract has committed to being judged on the metric it just introduced.

Exactness holds under real arithmetic. Floating-point summation is not associative, so reordering accumulation does not guarantee bit-identical output — only mathematically identical output. The paper claims the latter.
17§1 · one goal, two blockers

"Our main goal is to avoid reading and writing the attention matrix to and from HBM. This requires (i) computing the softmax reduction without access to the whole input (ii) not storing the large intermediate attention matrix for the backward pass."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §1 Introduction, p. 2
Reading

The cleanest problem statement in the paper, structured as goal → necessary conditions, not goal → wish list. Both must hold; either one failing puts the matrix back in HBM.

Obstacle (i) is Part 2, ¶06 restated. Softmax reduces over the whole row, so no partial view can produce a final weight. If you insist on seeing a whole row at once, and rows have length N, you are back to an N×N object.

Obstacle (ii) is Part 3, ¶15 restated. Even a perfectly fused forward pass must hand something to the backward pass, and the natural thing to hand it is S and P. Saving them undoes the entire forward win.

One goal, two independent blockers, two distinct techniques. Tiling answers (i); recomputation answers (ii). The paper never blurs them, and neither should you.
18§1 · the two techniques

"(i) We restructure the attention computation to split the input into blocks and make several passes over input blocks, thus incrementally performing the softmax reduction (also known as tiling). (ii) We store the softmax normalization factor from the forward pass to quickly recompute attention on-chip in the backward pass, which is faster than the standard approach of reading the intermediate attention matrix from HBM."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §1 Introduction, p. 2
Reading

"Restructure" is the honest verb. Tiling here is not "traverse the existing matrix in a cache-friendly order" — the matrix is never built. Each tile is produced, consumed, overwritten. That is the difference between a locality win and a materialization win, and only the latter changes the asymptotics of HBM traffic.

"Incrementally performing the softmax reduction" is the precise phrase for clearing obstacle (i). Not avoided, not approximated — performed, in pieces, with the pieces reconciled afterwards. That reconciliation is ¶05's scalar.

For (ii), note what gets stored: the normalization factor, not the matrix. A per-row scalar instead of a per-row vector of length N — an O(N2) save collapsed to an O(N) one.

4a · tiling

Making softmax composable

Four short paragraphs of algebra that turn ¶06's obstacle into ¶06's solution.

19§3.1 · the goal and the main idea

"Our goal is to reduce the amount of HBM accesses (to sub-quadratic in N). …

The main idea is that we split the inputs Q,K,V into blocks, load them from slow HBM to fast SRAM, then compute the attention output with respect to those blocks. By scaling the output of each block by the right normalization factor before adding them up, we get the correct result at the end."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §3.1, p. 4
Reading

The objective is finally stated in its own units: sub-quadratic HBM accesses. Not sub-quadratic FLOPs — the paper has already conceded it will use more.

The "main idea" sentence contains the whole algorithm and one clause carrying all the difficulty:

  • "split the inputs into blocks" — easy, and matmul already does it.
  • "compute the attention output with respect to those blocks" — a partial output, from a partial view of the row.
  • "by scaling the output of each block by the right normalization factor" — what is the right factor, and how can you know it before seeing the remaining blocks?

You cannot. The factor is not known until the row is finished. The resolution is to use a provisional factor and correct it retroactively — which is exactly ¶05, and exactly what ¶06's worked example showed happening naturally.

"Scaling by the right normalization factor" is not an implementation detail. It is the entire mathematical content of tiling; everything else is scheduling.
20§3.1 · the decomposition identity

"For vectors x(1),x(2)∈RB, we can decompose the softmax of the concatenated x=[x(1)x(2)]∈R2B as:

m(x)=max(m(x(1)),m(x(2))),
f(x)=[em(x(1))−m(x)f(x(1))em(x(2))−m(x)f(x(2))],
ℓ(x)=em(x(1))−m(x)ℓ(x(1))+em(x(2))−m(x)ℓ(x(2)),softmax(x)=f(x)ℓ(x).
arXiv:2205.14135 · §3.1 Tiling, p. 5
Reading

The mathematical heart of FlashAttention, and it says something simple: softmax statistics computed on two halves merge into the statistics of the whole, exactly, at the cost of two scalar multiplications.

  • The max merges trivially — max of a union is the max of the maxes. No correction needed.
  • The unnormalized exponentials merge with a per-half rescale. Half 1 was computed relative to its own max; the merged answer needs everything relative to the global max. Multiplying by em(x(1))−m(x) performs exactly that change of reference point — this is ¶05's identity, verbatim.
  • The denominator merges the same way, being a sum of the same terms.

Two structural facts make it usable. The correction is a scalar per half, not a vector, so fixing a whole block of accumulated work costs one multiply. And since m(x)≥m(x(k)) always, both factors lie in (0,1]: the correction can only scale down, never overflow.

Verify it. Take x(1)=[1,3] and x(2)=[5,2].

Per block: m(x(1))=3, f(x(1))=[0.1353,1], ℓ(x(1))=1.1353. And m(x(2))=5, f(x(2))=[1,0.0498], ℓ(x(2))=1.0498.

Merge: m(x)=5, so the factors are e3−5=0.1353 and e5−5=1:

ℓ(x)=0.1353×1.1353+1×1.0498=1.20344.

Direct, over all four entries:

e1−5+e3−5+e5−5+e2−5=0.018316+0.135335+1+0.049787=1.20344.

Identical. Block 1 had committed to a reference max of 3; block 2 raised it to 5; block 1's entire accumulated total was repaired with a single multiplication. Nothing had to be revisited.

21§3.1 · the consequence — constant state

"Therefore if we keep track of some extra statistics (m(x),ℓ(x)), we can compute softmax one block at a time.2 We thus split the inputs Q,K,V into blocks (Algorithm 1 line 3), compute the softmax values along with extra statistics (Algorithm 1 line 10), and combine the results (Algorithm 1 line 12)."

2"This style of aggregation is called algebraic aggregation [33]."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §3.1 Tiling, p. 5
Reading

"Some extra statistics" is an understated way to describe the payoff. The point is not that m and ℓ are cheap — it is that they are two scalars per row, and that number does not grow with how many blocks have been processed. The state is constant.

Constant state is what converts algebra into a kernel, because it is precisely the licence to overwrite scratch memory. If the carried state grew with the block count, you would have rebuilt P under another name. Instead, per query row you carry a max, a running denominator, and an output accumulator of length d. Processing block 47 needs nothing from blocks 1–46 except those numbers.

The footnote's term, algebraic aggregation, is the general principle: an aggregate is incrementally computable if it can be written as a bounded-size summary plus a merge function. A sum qualifies; a median does not. Softmax looks like it does not qualify — and ¶20 is the proof that with the right summary it does.

Part 2 said softmax refuses to be computed in pieces. That was true of the weights. It is false of the statistics, and the statistics are enough.

23§3.1 · recomputation

"Recomputation. … The backward pass typically requires the matrices S,P∈RN×N to compute the gradients with respect to Q,K,V. However, by storing the output O and the softmax normalization statistics (m,ℓ), we can recompute the attention matrix S and P easily in the backward pass from blocks of Q,K,V in SRAM. This can be seen as a form of selective gradient checkpointing [10, 34]."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §3.1 Recomputation, p. 5
Reading

The statistics (m,ℓ) were introduced in ¶21 to make the forward pass tileable. They turn out to be exactly what the backward pass needs too — not a coincidence, but the same fact viewed twice: those two scalars are a complete summary of the row's softmax, so anything derivable from the row is derivable from them plus the inputs.

The reconstruction is genuinely cheap. Given a tile Sij=QiKj⊤ recomputed on-chip plus the saved mi,ℓi, the probabilities come straight back as Pij=diag(ℓi)−1exp(Sij−mi). One matmul and one elementwise map, both in SRAM.

"Selective" is the important qualifier. Ordinary checkpointing drops activations at layer boundaries by a generic policy. Here the choice is surgical: drop precisely the two N×N matrices, keep precisely the O(N) summary that makes them cheap to rebuild.

What the backward pass is handed, per head per sample, at N=4096, d=64:

Saved tensorShapeBytes

O is excluded because every implementation keeps it anyway. The price is one extra QiKj⊤ per tile in the backward pass — roughly 30% more attention FLOPs, bought with a 2048× reduction in what must cross HBM.

24§3.1 · breaking the usual trade-off

"While gradient checkpointing has been suggested to reduce the maximum amount of memory required [66], all implementations (that we know off) have to trade speed for memory. In contrast, even with more FLOPs, our recomputation speeds up the backward pass due to reduced HBM accesses (Fig. 2)."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §3.1 Recomputation, p. 5
Reading

The paper deliberately positions itself against the thing it most resembles. Gradient checkpointing has a well-known price: recompute activations, spend more time, fit a bigger model. Everyone accepts it because there is no alternative.

FlashAttention's recomputation does not pay that price, and the sentence says why in six words: due to reduced HBM accesses. Recomputation happens in SRAM at ~19 TB/s; reading the saved matrix back happens from HBM at ~1.5 TB/s. Recomputing a value can be faster than remembering it, when remembering means a trip to slow memory.

This is the cleanest demonstration of what the IO cost model buys. Under a FLOP model, "recompute instead of store" is unambiguously a cost and the only question is how much. Under an IO model it can be better than free.

The memory-versus-speed trade-off is an artifact of assuming arithmetic is expensive and memory is free. Invert that assumption and the trade-off inverts with it.
25§3.1 · kernel fusion, now that it works

"Implementation details: Kernel fusion. Tiling enables us to implement our algorithm in one CUDA kernel, loading input from HBM, performing all the computation steps (matrix multiply, softmax, optionally masking and dropout, matrix multiply), then write the result back to HBM. This avoids repeatedly reading and writing of inputs and outputs from and to HBM."

arXiv:2205.14135 · §3.1, p. 5
Reading

Compare against ¶15, where fusion was introduced only to be declared insufficient. Nothing about fusion has changed; what changed is that both objections were removed.

  • Fusion needed the whole softmax row on-chip — tiling removed that requirement (¶21).
  • Fusion still had to write intermediates for backward — recomputation removed that requirement (¶23).

So "tiling enables us to implement our algorithm in one CUDA kernel" is a precise causal statement. The single kernel is the payoff of §3.1, not its method.

And note the list inside the parentheses: matmul, softmax, masking, dropout, matmul. Those are the five stacked bars in Figure 1's right panel — five kernels, therefore at least five HBM round trips. They are now one.

Algorithm 1, line by line

26Algorithm 1 · lines 1–4 — SRAM sets the block size
Algorithm 1 — FlashAttention (lines 1–4)

Reading

The Require line alone is remarkable. A hardware capacity, M, appears as a formal input to an algorithm. Most algorithms are stated independently of the machine; this one cannot be, because its purpose is to fit a working set into a specific tier of memory. The IO-awareness thesis has become a function signature.

Line 1 is where M gets spent — and the two block sizes come out different, because Bc and Br do not buy the same thing.

1 · Where the 4d comes from. Four block-shaped matrices must be resident simultaneously:

Resident in SRAMShapeScales with
Kj, VjBc×d eachBc
Qi, OiBr×d eachBr

Four matrices, a quarter of M each, d columns apiece — hence ⌈M/4d⌉ rows.

2 · The cap at d is derived, not chosen. There is a fifth resident object those four omit: the score tile Sij, whose size is the product Br×Bc. That is the term that can explode. Require it to fit in a quarter too:

BrBc≤M4⟺Br⋅M4d≤M4⟺Br≤d

So d is exactly the largest query block for which the score tile still fits. Without the cap, doubling SRAM would quadruple the tile.

3 · Does it fit in practice? An A100 SM has 192 KB of SRAM — in fp16, M=98,304 elements. With d=64:

Bc=⌈983044×64⌉=384,Br=min(384,64)=64
ResidentShapeSize (fp16)

It fits, with room for m,ℓ and register spill — and the tile lands at 24 576 elements against a quarter-budget of 24 576, to the element. Against the matrix it replaces, 4096×4096=16,777,216, the tile is 683× smaller. That is why one lives in SRAM and the other cannot.

4 · Only Bc buys anything. Count the traffic. K,V are read once each overall; Q and O are re-read and re-written on every outer iteration, which dominates:

Tc⋅Nd=NBc⋅Nd=N2dM/4d=4N2d2M

Br does not appear. Enlarging Bc cuts HBM traffic; enlarging Br cuts nothing — it only changes tile shape and the parallelism each block exposes. So SRAM is spent making Bc as large as possible and Br gets what is left. That one line is also where §3.2's O(N2d2M−1) comes from.

The upshot: the tile is deliberately short and wide — 64 rows × 384 columns, not square. Wide in the key axis because that is what cuts passes over Q; short in the query axis because height is capped by the tile budget and buys nothing anyway.

These are order-of-magnitude sizings for the analysis, not a tight fit. In the regime that matters — M>4d2, i.e. 98 304 > 16 384 — the min binds and everything lands comfortably. When M≤4d2 the four tensors alone already claim all of M and the accounting is loose. Production kernels use hand-tuned block sizes, not this formula.

Line 11 is ¶20's identity, transliterated. The paper's ℓ(x)=em(x(1))−m(x)ℓ(x(1))+em(x(2))−m(x)ℓ(x(2)) is line 11 with "block 1" renamed to everything seen so far and "block 2" renamed to the tile in hand. Applied Tc times in sequence; exact each time, therefore exact overall.

Line 12 packs three separate ideas into one expression:

  • diag(ℓi)Oi — un-normalize. Oi currently holds an average divided by the old denominator. Multiplying by ℓi recovers the raw weighted sum, the only form that can be added to.
  • emi−minew and em˜ij−minew — re-base. Old accumulation and new tile sit on different maxima; these two scalars put both on the new common scale. This is ¶05, and it is the "÷45" column from ¶06's table.
  • diag(ℓinew)−1 — re-normalize. Divide by the updated denominator so Oi is again a valid weighted average over every key seen so far.

The critical invariant: after every single iteration, Oi is the exact attention output for the subset of keys processed to date. There is no finalization step — line 16 just returns, because the last iteration already left the answer correct.

The rescale must hit ℓ and the accumulator together. Correcting the denominator while leaving the numerator on the old scale gives a silently wrong answer that still looks like a probability-weighted average. Lines 11 and 12 use the same two exponentials for exactly this reason.
Because line 12 divides every iteration, this version normalizes Tc times per query block instead of once at the end. Redundant arithmetic — cheap in the IO model, and FlashAttention-2 removes it by deferring the division to the very end (¶32).

30Theorem 1 · what has been proved, and what has not

"Theorem 1. Algorithm 1 returns O=softmax(QK⊤)V with O(N2d) FLOPs and requires O(N) additional memory beyond inputs and output."

arXiv:2205.14135 · Theorem 1, p. 5
Reading

Three claims — and note which one is not here.

  • Correctness. The returned value is written as the definition itself, with an equals sign. Not "approximately". This formally discharges the word "exact" from ¶16.
  • O(N2d) FLOPs. Identical to standard attention — exactly as ¶03 predicted, since the compute term was never schedule-dependent. Constant factors are higher because of recomputation; the order is unchanged.
  • O(N) additional memory. The headline. Quadratic scratch → linear scratch. "Additional… beyond inputs and output" is careful phrasing: the O(Nd) for Q,K,V,O is not counted because it is not scratch. What FlashAttention adds is just m and ℓ: 2N numbers.

What it conspicuously does not claim: anything about speed, or about HBM accesses. Correctness and memory are settled here; the IO count — the metric the whole paper is built on — is deliberately held back for §3.2, so it can be stated together with its matching lower bound.

Theorem 1 closes the exactness question and the memory question, and leaves the IO question open on purpose. That is the cliff-edge this read stops at.

What changed in FlashAttention-2, -3 and -4

FlashAttention-1 removed the memory wall. Everything after it is a consequence of that: once HBM traffic stops being the binding constraint, whatever was hiding behind it becomes the bottleneck — and each successor attacks the next one down.

The framing shifts. FA1 asked "how many bytes cross HBM"; FA2 asks "what fraction of cycles are spent on the tensor cores". On an A100 a non-matmul FLOP costs roughly 16× a matmul FLOP in opportunity terms, so even a small amount of elementwise work between matmuls is expensive.

Deferring the division. ¶28's caveat was that Algorithm 1 line 12 divides by ℓinew on every iteration — Tc divisions where one would do. FA2 keeps the accumulator unnormalized and divides once after the loop:

O˜i←emiold−minewO˜i+em˜ij−minewP˜ijVj,Oi=diag(ℓi)−1O˜i at the end

This is legal for exactly the reason ¶05 gave: normalization is a scalar that commutes with the accumulation, so it can be postponed.

Swapping the loops. ¶27 measured the cost of FA1's order — O read and written Tc=11 times, 11 MiB of the 18 MiB total. Put Q outside and the accumulator never leaves registers; Oi is written exactly once. That single change removes most of the traffic that ¶27's table showed FA1 still paying.

Parallelizing over sequence length. FA1 assigns one thread block per (batch, head). With long sequences you use a small batch, so on a 108-SM A100 you can simply run out of blocks and leave most of the GPU idle. FA2 also splits the query axis across blocks — which the loop swap makes free, since query blocks are already independent (¶02).

Split-Q instead of split-K. Inside a block, FA1 divided K/V across warps with Q shared, so every warp held a partial result for the same output rows and had to round-trip through shared memory to combine them. FA2 divides Q across warps with K/V shared: each warp owns its own output rows outright, and the shared-memory reduction disappears.

Not one of these changes alters what is computed. They are all about removing work that was never the answer — divisions, redundant writes, idle SMs, warp synchronization.

The asynchrony problem. Hopper's tensor cores (WGMMA) and its memory engine (TMA) can both run asynchronously — issue the work, do something else, collect it later. A synchronous kernel written in FA2's style leaves that capability unused: it loads, then computes, then loads. FA3 splits the warps into producers that only move data and consumers that only do math, passing tiles through a circular buffer in shared memory. Loading tile j+1 now happens underneath the math for tile j.

The softmax problem, which is Part 3 all over again. The exponentials run on the special-function unit, whose throughput is a small fraction of the tensor cores'. So during softmax the most expensive silicon on the chip sits idle. This is the same shape of complaint as ¶12 — a low-intensity operation stalling a high-throughput one — but now inside the fused kernel rather than between kernels. FA3's answer is to schedule two warpgroups out of phase, so one is doing exp while the other is doing QK⊤.

The precision problem. FP8 doubles tensor-core throughput but has ~2–3 decimal digits of precision, and attention scores have wide dynamic range. Two mitigations: block quantization, so each tile gets its own scale factor rather than one scale for the whole tensor; and incoherent processing, which multiplies Q and K by a random orthogonal (Hadamard) matrix first. That leaves QK⊤ unchanged — orthogonal matrices cancel — while spreading outliers across coordinates so no single value dominates its block's scale. Reported ≈2.6× lower numerical error than baseline FP8 attention.

FA3 is genuinely Hopper-only. Warp specialization, TMA and WGMMA are architecture features, not optimizations — the kernel does not degrade gracefully on older cards, it does not run.

Two of these are worth understanding even without confirmation, because they are natural consequences of everything above.

Attacking exp directly. FA3 hid the special-function unit's latency behind matmuls. The next move, once it is still the limiter, is to stop using it: evaluate ex with a short polynomial on the ordinary arithmetic units instead. This is viable precisely because of ¶04 — after subtracting the row max, every argument lies in (−∞,0] and the values that matter are in a narrow band near zero. A general-purpose exp is over-engineered for that range.

Skipping the rescale. Look again at ¶05: the correction factor is emold−mnew, which equals exactly 1 whenever the new tile fails to beat the running maximum. In a long sequence the max is usually established early, so most tiles trigger a multiplication by 1 across the whole accumulator. Detecting that and branching past it is free correctness — the ¶06 worked example is the rare case, not the common one.

Do not read this as "FA4 changed the algorithm". Skipping a multiplication by 1 and swapping in a different exp routine are implementation choices. The recurrence in ¶20 is untouched.

Reading

Four years, three successor releases, and the recurrence of ¶20 is untouched. Every subsequent version is a scheduling paper: which loop is outside, which warp does what, when the division happens, which functional unit evaluates exp. The mathematics was finished in 2022.

That is the real lesson of the sequence, and it justifies having spent Parts 1 and 2 on softmax rather than on GPUs. The durable contribution was noticing that softmax is algebraically aggregable (¶21) — that a bounded summary plus a merge rule is enough. Everything since has been engineering against a moving hardware target, and it will keep moving.

Learn ¶20 and ¶21 properly and you understand all four versions. The rest is which chip you are on this year.

Closing

Softmax refuses to be computed in pieces — but only up to a scalar. Bytes moved, not operations performed, is what the machine charges for. Put those two facts together and FlashAttention is not a trick; it is the only reasonable thing to do.

Six questions to check yourself
  1. Why can the quadratic memory term be removed while the quadratic compute term cannot?
  2. In ¶06, why was every old weight divided by the same factor?
  3. Why does the paper say softmax "couples columns of K" rather than "couples rows of Q"?
  4. Why is the rescale factor guaranteed to lie in (0,1], and why does that matter?
  5. Where does the 4 in Bc=⌈M/4d⌉ come from, and what does min(⋅,d) protect?
  6. Line 12 writes Oi to HBM every inner iteration. Why is that not a contradiction of the goal?
